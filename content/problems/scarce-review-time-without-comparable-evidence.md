# What happens today

The model can source, attract, screen, and select clinicians, but it collapses the human work around those stages into one review path. Human effort can be spent locating and enriching evidence, interacting with a clinician to generate evidence that does not exist in static records, resolving ambiguity, or making a consequential selection and contracting decision.

At low volume one person or team may do all of this. At scale the same work may be specialized across internal teams, external partners, or different levels of expertise. The operating problem is therefore not simply that a person reviews each prospect. It is that the system does not distinguish which kind of human attention is needed, what evidence should already be assembled before that person enters, or how the resulting context moves to the next decision owner.

# Why it matters

A uniform human path lets high-volume evidence work consume the same capacity needed for harder clinical and network judgments. Automating indiscriminately creates the opposite failure: a prospect can experience the platform as efficient but impersonal, opaque, or dismissive of their expertise.

Specializing the work only helps if the evidence record survives the handoff. Otherwise each team reconstructs the candidate from source material, the clinician repeats information, and later decisions cannot teach the system which earlier evidence was actually useful.

# Open questions

- Which evidence can the system gather, verify, normalize, or request without specialist human judgment?
- Which interactions are worth qualified human time because they generate meaningful new evidence about how a clinician behaves?
- What shared evidence record lets qualification work hand off cleanly to consequential selection and contracting decisions?
- Which final selection decisions depend on network supply and demand context, and how should that context stay separate from the definition of clinician quality?
